    Good to know

    • Nothing you see here is saved or sent. Your settings are remembered in this browser; the passwords aren't.
    • Use a different password for every account, and keep them in a password manager (the one built into your browser or phone is fine). Then you only need to remember one strong passphrase.
    • Copied passwords stay on your clipboard until you copy something else, and some systems keep a clipboard history (on Windows, press Win+V to see and clear it).
    • Turn on two-step sign-in where you can. It protects you even if a password leaks.
    How strength is calculated

    Strength is shown in bits: each extra bit doubles the number of possible results. Because every character or word here is picked at random, the count is exact: for example, 16 characters from 87 possible ones give 8716 possibilities, about 103 bits. With "at least one of each kind", the passwords missing a kind are subtracted from the count.

    The figure only holds for passwords made randomly like this. A password you invent yourself, like Summer2024!, is far weaker than its length suggests, because attackers try common patterns first.

    A passphrase's strength comes from the number of words, not their length: each word adds about 12.9 bits (7,772 possible words; the list's 4 hyphenated words are left out so a dashed phrase can only be read one way). Capitalizing every word adds no strength, since an attacker can assume it.

    Guessing times assume someone stole a site's password database and tries guesses on their own computers, finding yours on average halfway through. The speeds are rough: real ones vary a lot with the hardware and how the site protected the passwords. A site's login page is much slower to attack, because it limits attempts.

    Randomness comes from your browser's cryptographic generator (crypto.getRandomValues), the same one used for secure connections.
